**Build Rehearsal: test a factory’s recovery plan before changing the shift.**

Decision by the independent project-selection research agent, October 14, 2026. Working product name; no trademark clearance implied. This is a recommendation and evaluation design, not a claim that the product or its results already exist. The decision respects the event-provided $100 AMD allocation and up to $20 additional personal spending. Team size and assumed working hours were not used to eliminate ambitious ideas.

**The choice is a recovery decision product for production planners at make-to-order manufacturers.** When a machine goes down or an urgent order arrives, Rehearsal turns the current job schedule and an operational message into a checked recovery packet: which work moves, which delivery promises remain achievable, what overtime or other permission is needed, and which simulated futures cause each option to fail. A planner inspects the evidence, approves a revision, and exports the revised dispatch list. Enter **Intelligent Industry**.

The one-sentence pitch: **“Your machine stopped. Rehearsal shows which orders you can still promise—and tests the recovery before you commit.”**

I recommend this over maintenance detection, electronics substitution, recall matching, and pure inference infrastructure because its most valuable result is independently testable with accessible data, visually understandable, commercially specific, and capable of substantive AMD execution. This is a judgment about the strongest evidence-backed submission we can produce, not a prediction of a win or a claim that scheduling is a new market.

**The buyer receives a decision they can act on.** The initial user is a production planner or shift supervisor at a high-mix machine shop; the buyer is the plant or operations manager. Start with discrete jobs, multiple eligible machines, operation precedence, and a current shift. These users must reconcile job priorities, unavailable resources, due dates, and work already in progress whenever circumstances change. Siemens’s own case material describes manufacturers depending on planner knowledge and manually absorbing disruptions; this validates the problem class, while also establishing formidable existing competition. We do not have a customer interview or evidence that this particular buyer would purchase our product. [Siemens’s manufacturing scheduling case](https://blogs.sw.siemens.com/opcenter/partner-of-the-month-lean-scheduling-brasil-unlocking-hidden-capacity-with-opcenter-aps/)

The first useful input bundle is deliberately concrete: CSV/JSON jobs and machine capabilities; the currently committed schedule; a shift calendar; a short maintenance or production message; and user-supplied commercial priorities. These are materials a real planner could export, but the hackathon fixtures will be explicitly labeled benchmark-derived or synthetic. No proprietary ERP, factory connection, private dataset, sensor purchase, or paid information feed is essential.

The packet must contain a before/after schedule, an exact change list, the checked constraints and their sources, affected orders, alternative plans, separately labeled scenario assumptions, and an approval record. Its operational output is a revised dispatch CSV/JSON and an explanation of the concessions authorized. A working local approval/export flow is sufficient to demonstrate the product; a future ERP connector is not an implemented feature.

**The distinctive capability is an auditable recovery decision from an imperfect operational message.** It comprises four connected behaviors:

1. Extract a typed change request with source spans: affected machine, outage interval, changed due date, priority, or authorized overtime. Surface unresolved identifiers, missing units, contradictions, and uncertain times before they silently enter the plan.
2. Produce and independently validate alternatives against the accepted factory state. Preserve finished work and explicitly model what can happen to work in progress. An LLM cannot invent machine compatibility, alter a deadline, or authorize overtime.
3. Rehearse each alternative under the same declared uncertainty scenarios. Show a concrete failing timeline as well as aggregate outcomes, so the planner can understand the weak point in an attractive option.
4. When the requested combination cannot be satisfied, identify the conflicting restrictions and show feasible alternatives with explicit concessions. A due date is never silently relaxed to make a green dashboard.

The public contribution is the complete, reproducible chain from source message to accepted constraints, checked alternatives, replay, and authorized revision. This is a product and evaluation contribution, not a claim to have invented optimization, explainability, digital twins, or stochastic scheduling. Call the model a **schedule simulator**, rather than implying that a calibrated digital twin of a real factory exists.

**The memorable demo is one broken promise exposed before it reaches the shop floor.** Use an attractive, legible factory timeline with a small number of machines and orders. The numbers below describe input fixtures and interactions, not measured outcomes:

1. Show the current shift and its delivery commitments. Drop in a note such as: “Mill M2 is unavailable until 14:00. Order J7 still needs to ship at 16:00. Overtime requires approval.”
2. Highlight exactly where each changed constraint came from. The model links “Mill M2” to the known asset; an ambiguous name or unsupported time expression produces a visible review request.
3. Display checked recovery alternatives. One protects the nominal due date with little change; another uses an already qualified alternate machine; a third requests a priced overtime window. Only include capabilities and permissions actually present in the fixture.
4. Change the repair assumption or select **Show a failure**. Replay one sampled future in which the appealing nominal plan misses J7. Show the operation that creates the bottleneck. The other plan’s tradeoff is immediately visible.
5. Tighten the requirements until they conflict. Show an honest infeasibility result or “no feasible plan found within the search budget,” whichever the solver actually establishes. Offer an explicitly authorized concession, then approve and export the chosen revision.
6. Open a compact run inspector: AMD inference trace, actual GPU/ROCm replay record, constraint-check results, benchmark sample counts, and measured latency. Finish with the resulting dispatch list and the business decision.

This demo should work with a judge’s changed outage duration or due date. A preselected counterexample can support the video, but arbitrary input must exercise the real pipeline. Never imply that prerecorded traces are fresh execution.

**The architecture gives AI a consequential job and gives numerical claims an independent check.**

| Stage | What generates the result | What checks it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Message to constraint proposal | Open language model served on AMD | Schema, identifiers, units, source-span existence, contradiction rules, human confirmation of material ambiguities |
| Candidate schedules | Conventional CP-SAT optimization on CPU | A separate schedule checker for precedence, eligibility, resource overlap, availability, release times, and committed work |
| Uncertainty analysis | Batched schedule replay in PyTorch on an AMD GPU | CPU reference replay using the same sampled inputs; independent outcome recomputation |
| Infeasibility/concessions | Solver statuses and explicit permitted relaxations | Validate each alternative; distinguish a sufficient conflict set from a proven minimum conflict set |
| Explanation and approval packet | Templates plus AMD model explanation of computed facts | Render numeric values from computation; source links and reproducible run artifacts; human approval |

Source-span existence is not proof that an extraction has the correct meaning. Semantic extraction accuracy needs its own labeled evaluation. Likewise, a valid schedule proves consistency with the encoded constraints, not that the factory model includes every real-world constraint. Keep both limits visible.

Use a narrow typed constraint language with supported operations, not unrestricted model-generated solver code. Google’s official OR-Tools documentation and flexible-job-shop example establish the underlying solver approach. The team’s work is integrating the business semantics, state-preserving repair, alternatives, replay, and user decision. The second AMD workload is many independent replays of candidate schedules under uncertain processing or recovery times. Implement the supported replay semantics as batched tensor operations and compare them to a CPU reference. PyTorch explicitly supports HIP through its existing GPU tensor interfaces. This verifies a feasible programming route, not that this workload will outperform a competent CPU implementation. [PyTorch HIP documentation](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/2.14/notes/hip.html)

The benefit of this stage is **evidence about a decision’s sensitivity**, even before a speed advantage is measured. The mandatory AMD contribution remains real inference that turns the unstructured disruption into constraints used by the product. Pre-register an evaluation with these components before tuning:

- **Message understanding:** target 120 independently reviewed messages split into development and held-out sets, grouped by scenario/template family. Include aliases, time units, negation, conflicting updates, unknown machines, and missing information. One member drafts, the other labels independently; adjudicate disagreements before evaluating. Report complete-change accuracy, unsupported fields, critical omissions, false acceptance, and useful abstention. These counts are proposed, not completed.
- **Recovery quality:** use at least 30 held-out base instances/event streams across several sizes and difficulty levels. Report feasibility, weighted tardiness, schedule disruption, explicitly priced overtime, solve time, and solver status/bound. Hold the available information and decision horizon constant. Do not expose future event realizations to our planner while withholding them from baselines.
- **Strong baselines:** a sensible dispatch rule; a deterministic CP-SAT replan given human-verified structured changes; a single-pass version of the same AMD model feeding the same solver; and the full product. The CPU solver with clean inputs is the competent reference, not a straw man that the LLM is expected to beat.
- **Ablations:** remove source/semantic checks, remove uncertainty replay, and remove state-preservation penalties separately. Measure whether each feature improves its intended outcome. Do not claim the model discovered better schedules when the improvement came from the solver.
- **Replay validity and uncertainty:** compare CPU and GPU results on identical sampled tensors, including boundary and failure cases. Separate scenario samples used to select an option from fresh samples used to estimate its outcomes. Preserve declared correlations where modeled. Report sample counts, sampling error, and sensitivity to distribution assumptions. If only bounds are supplied, show bounded scenarios rather than inventing probabilities.
- **Hardware and workflow:** time optimized CPU replay against AMD replay at useful batch sizes, including synchronization and data transfer. Record cold/warm latency and the complete request time. Separately measure minutes to a correct approved packet with and without the product; a test performed only by the builders must be labeled accordingly.

For replay, fix the candidate’s dispatch policy before sampling. Future durations may delay work, but the simulator must not secretly re-optimize each future with perfect foresight. Define breakdown behavior for in-progress work explicitly; initially the supported subset can use non-preemptive operations and outages that begin between operations. Unsupported interruption/rework cases must request review. No “95% reliable” claim is justified merely because 95% of synthetic draws succeed: display “X of N simulated scenarios under these assumptions.”

Our competitive evidence would be fewer incorrectly accepted change requests, fewer model-constraint violations, faster preparation of a valid decision packet, and better declared risk/cost tradeoffs in held-out simulations. Real downtime avoided, production safety, revenue preserved, and enterprise readiness remain unmeasured until field validation.

**The decisive uncertainties are explicit.** The project is selected; these are verification targets rather than reasons to defer the decision:

- The deployed AMD model may be poor at precise temporal changes or source-grounded extraction. Test difficult held-out cases early; use a stronger available AMD model or a structured confirmation interface if needed. Generic free-form reasoning is not the essential feature.
- The GPU replay implementation may be slower than optimized CPU replay at relevant scales. Preserve the correct simulation, publish the result honestly, and keep AMD inference substantive. Claim acceleration only if measured.
- Synthetic manufacturing data supports scheduling correctness and controlled experiments, not actual factory adoption or calibrated downtime probabilities. Any future domain interview strengthens business evidence but is not a hidden prerequisite for this recommendation.
- A competent solver may show that all candidate plans have similar outcomes. That is a valid result. Evaluate broader held-out disruptions and report the distribution; do not cherry-pick a dramatic fabricated win.
- Judges may find the product insufficiently original given existing APS systems. The concrete differentiator, visible counterexample, reproducibility, and source-grounded checks are essential—not optional polish.
